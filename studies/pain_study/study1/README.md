# Materials and Methods

## Research Objectives
The primary objective of this study was to determine whether the continuous, trial-wise expression of established functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) pain signatures can be predicted above chance from concurrent EEG-derived features. The predictive targets were the Neurologic Pain Signature (NPS; Wager et al., 2013) and the Stimulus Intensity Independent Pain Signature-1 (SIIPS1; Woo et al., 2017), modeled via a subject-level, trial-resolved multimodal architecture. The primary confirmatory estimand was prospective, leave-one-subject-out prediction of stimulus-controlled signature expression: NPS and SIIPS1 dot-product scores residualized against prespecified stimulus and session variables, without using any distributional statistic from the held-out subject. Raw signature prediction was retained as a descriptive upper-bound analysis, and subjective-rating residualization was treated as a construct-attenuation sensitivity analysis rather than as a nuisance-controlled primary result.

The NPS was developed as a brain-based marker of nociceptive processing (Wager et al., 2013), and its prediction may partly reflect stimulus intensity and nociceptive input. The SIIPS1 was designed to capture stimulus-intensity-independent pain-related variance (Woo et al., 2017), making it conceptually closer to the residualized objective. SIIPS1 was nevertheless evaluated under the same residualization levels to verify independence in this dataset rather than assuming it from prior validation. All analyses were restricted to the plateau phase of thermal stimulation periods, including pain and non-pain thermal trials, utilizing fMRI inputs normalized to the MNI152NLin2009cAsym standard space.

## Data Preconditions and Frequency Definitions
Analyses relied on task-evoked EEG epochs defined from $-7.0$ to $15.0$ seconds relative to stimulus onset. A $-0.2$ to $0.0$ seconds pre-stimulus baseline was applied to epoch-level voltage to remove DC offset prior to ERP and amplitude-based analyses. For time-frequency decompositions, the primary log-ratio baseline correction used the $-5.0$ to $-0.01$ seconds pre-stimulus window. This window was selected to provide stable power estimates across the low-frequency bands, as Morlet wavelet decomposition at alpha frequencies requires several cycles for adequate spectral resolution. Because this window may contain cue-locked expectancy activity, baseline sensitivity analyses were prespecified using two alternative windows: an immediate pre-stimulus baseline from $-0.2$ to $-0.01$ seconds and an early pre-stimulus baseline from $-7.0$ to $-5.5$ seconds. The early-window analysis was interpreted only when event timing confirmed that it preceded task cues. Baseline conclusions were considered stable only if the sign and inferential status of the primary alpha and beta effects were unchanged across the primary and immediate-baseline analyses. All baseline windows were applied identically across pain and non-pain conditions.

The active stimulation window utilized for predictive modeling was restricted to $3.0$ to $10.5$ seconds post-onset. Neural oscillations were operationalized into standard frequency bands: alpha ($8.0$–$12.9$ Hz), beta ($13.0$–$30.0$ Hz), and gamma ($30.1$–$80.0$ Hz). Composite features spanning the full alpha-to-gamma spectrum were also constructed by concatenating these distinct ranges. Gamma-band EEG recorded during simultaneous fMRI is vulnerable to facial muscle activity, jaw tension, scanner gradient residuals, and cardiac artifacts, all of which can be elicited by painful stimulation. Gamma-band analyses were therefore exploratory by design and were not used to support confirmatory pain-signature claims. Gamma robustness was evaluated quantitatively using temporal/frontal high-frequency electromyographic power, framewise displacement, DVARS, cardiac phase-locking, and residual spectral peaks at the volume-repetition frequency or its harmonics. Prespecified artifact thresholds were framewise displacement $> 0.5$ mm, temporal/frontal high-frequency power robust $z > 3$, DVARS robust $z > 3$, cardiac phase-locking above the 95th percentile of the within-subject circular-shift null, or scanner-frequency residual peaks robust $z > 3$. Gamma effects were reported as artifact-sensitive when removing threshold-exceeding trials changed the effect direction or eliminated significance. The gamma band structurally excludes a $\pm 1.0$ Hz notch around 60 Hz due to line-noise removal; harmonics at 120 Hz and above fall outside the 100 Hz low-pass cutoff and are therefore not relevant.

## Simultaneous EEG-fMRI Preprocessing
Simultaneous EEG-fMRI acquisition introduces structured artifacts that must be addressed prior to feature extraction (Bullock et al., 2021). Preprocessing was conducted in two stages. In the first stage, MRI-induced artifacts were corrected using BrainVision Analyzer 2.0 (Brain Products GmbH, Gilching, Germany). Gradient artifacts arising from the rapid switching of magnetic field gradients during echo-planar imaging were removed using the average artifact subtraction method, in which a sliding-window template of the gradient waveform was estimated and subtracted from each volume acquisition window. Ballistocardiogram artifacts—caused by pulse-related electrode motion within the static magnetic field—were corrected using the same software via template subtraction aligned to the detected cardiac cycle.

In the second stage, the gradient- and BCG-corrected data were imported into the MNE-BIDS-Pipeline for standard EEG preprocessing. Continuous data were downsampled from the acquisition rate to 500 Hz. A high-pass filter at 0.1 Hz and a low-pass filter at 100 Hz were applied. Line noise at 60 Hz was attenuated using a notch filter.

Bad channels were identified using PyPREP (Bigdely-Shamlo et al., 2015), which applies automated detection criteria including deviation-based and correlation-based rejection. RANSAC validation was optionally enabled. To improve robustness, bad-channel detection was repeated three times with independent random seeds, and a channel was marked bad only if flagged in a strict majority of runs. Detected bad channels were synchronized across runs within each subject using a per-run union policy, then interpolated using spherical spline interpolation. The data were subsequently re-referenced to the common average.

Independent component analysis was performed using the extended infomax algorithm with 0.99 variance explained as the component retention criterion. ICA was fitted on epochs high-pass filtered at 1.0 Hz to improve decomposition stability. Component classification was performed using ICLabel (Pion-Tonachini et al., 2019), and components were rejected if their predicted probability exceeded 0.8 for any non-brain category other than "other" (i.e., only components classified as "brain" or "other" were retained). Because ICLabel was trained on standard (non-fMRI) EEG data, residual gradient artifact fragments, BCG template-subtraction residuals, and scanner-related cable-motion artifacts may be classified as "other" and retained. As a supplementary quality-control step, retained components were inspected for spectral peaks at the volume-repetition frequency or its harmonics, for cardiac phase-locking exceeding chance, and for spatial topographies consistent with known artifact patterns. The number of rejected components per subject and their ICLabel classifications were logged.

Epochs were extracted from $-7.0$ to $15.0$ seconds relative to stimulus onset. Trial rejection was performed using autoreject (Jas et al., 2017) in local mode, which determines channel-specific peak-to-peak amplitude thresholds via cross-validation and repairs or rejects individual channels within individual epochs. The number of candidate interpolations per trial was set to $\{4, 8, 16\}$. The number of rejected and interpolated trials per subject was logged. Electrode locations were digitized using the EasyCap M1 montage and co-registered to individual MRI.

## fMRI Signature Target Construction
Trial-by-trial fMRI effect estimates were extracted using the Least-Squares Separate (LSS) generalized linear modeling approach, restricted to stimulation-phase trials during the thermal plateau period. The LSS event regressor onset was defined as the beginning of the thermal plateau (after ramp-up), with event duration equal to the plateau hold time. Ramp-up and ramp-down periods were modeled as separate nuisance regressors within the LSS framework to prevent their variance from contaminating plateau-specific estimates. All remaining non-stimulation trial types were similarly modeled as nuisance regressors. This design aligns the modeled fMRI event with the same protocol-defined plateau phase used for EEG feature extraction, but the resulting LSS beta is an HRF-convolved event-amplitude estimate and was not interpreted as temporally equivalent to millisecond-scale EEG activity. The primary LSS models utilized a canonical SPM hemodynamic response function, a cosine drift model, and a high-pass filter of $0.008$ Hz, without additional spatial smoothing. HRF/timing robustness was evaluated by repeating the target construction with canonical-HRF temporal and dispersion derivatives and by testing a finite-impulse-response model centered on the plateau; EEG prediction was also repeated with shifted active windows displaced by $\pm 2.0$ seconds from the primary $3.0$ to $10.5$ seconds window. A primary result was considered HRF/timing-stable only if the Level 2 ElasticNet estimate retained the same sign for both co-primary targets, the absolute performance estimate changed by no more than 25% relative to the primary analysis, and the Holm-corrected inferential status was unchanged. Results failing any of these criteria were reported as HRF/timing-sensitive and could not support the primary physiological interpretation. The NPS weight map was the group-level cross-validated principal component regression weights (`weights_NSF_grouppred_cvpcr.nii.gz`; Wager et al., 2013) and the SIIPS1 map was the 137-subject weighted mean (`nonnoc_v11_4_137subjmap_weighted_mean.nii.gz`; Woo et al., 2017), both registered to MNI152NLin2009cAsym space.

When image grids differed, LSS beta maps were resampled to the signature-weight grid using continuous interpolation, and brain masks were resampled with nearest-neighbor interpolation. For each signature, the scoring mask $V^{(k)}$ was fixed by the finite signature-weight voxels intersected with the resampled fMRIPrep brain mask, and the contributing voxel count was required to be identical across all retained subjects, runs, and trials for that signature. A trial was retained for the primary analysis only when every voxel in this fixed mask had a finite LSS beta value. Trials with missing or non-finite beta values inside $V^{(k)}$ failed target quality control rather than being scored on a smaller trial-specific voxel set, and any variation in the logged voxel count failed target construction. The fixed contributing voxel count was logged for every target, and any subject whose QC exclusions left insufficient retained trials failed analysis-level inclusion. LSS beta maps were not spatially smoothed, z-scored, or trial-normalized before signature scoring, and signature weights were not re-estimated from the study data. Let $\beta_{s,i}(v)$ denote the LSS-derived blood-oxygen-level-dependent effect estimate for subject $s$, trial $i$, and voxel $v$, and let $M_k(v)$ denote the a priori spatial weight map for target $k \in \{\text{NPS}, \text{SIIPS1}\}$. The primary signature-expression metric was computed as the unscaled dot product across the fixed masked voxel set $V^{(k)}$:

$$y_{s,i}^{(k)} = \sum_{v \in V^{(k)}} \beta_{s,i}(v) \, M_k(v)$$

To construct the multimodal dataset, the resultant fMRI targets $y_{s,i}^{(k)}$ were matched to clean EEG trials using unique subject, run/block, and trial-index identifiers. Exact trial identifiers were required for confirmatory alignment; missing identifiers, duplicate identifiers, or one-to-many EEG-fMRI matches failed fast. Rounded onset-duration agreement was retained only as an audit check after trial-identifier matching. EEG trigger onsets and fMRI plateau onsets were compared after applying the prespecified protocol offset from trigger to plateau start; the residual absolute mismatch had to be $\leq 0.010$ seconds for every retained trial. Any trial exceeding this threshold was excluded, and any run with more than 1% excluded temporal-audit failures or any maximum residual mismatch $>0.050$ seconds failed run-level alignment QC. Temporal mismatch audits did not create a fallback matching route or justify averaging duplicate trials.

## Hierarchical Nuisance Residualization
To characterize whether EEG prediction reflects pain-relevant fMRI variance or confound-correlated variance, fMRI signature expression was evaluated at three prespecified residualization levels. These levels are distinct estimands rather than interchangeable preprocessing options. A level was analyzed only when its nuisance design was full rank within every training fold; rank-deficient designs were treated as unidentified and failed fast rather than being silently simplified.

**Level 1 — Raw expression.** No residualization. Models predict the full NPS/SIIPS1 dot-product expression, which reflects stimulus intensity, condition, temporal structure, subjective experience, and neural pain processing in an unknown mixture.

**Level 2 — Stimulus-controlled expression.** The targets were residualized against a design matrix containing an intercept, dummy-coded stimulus temperature levels (`stimulus_temp`, with the lowest observed retained temperature as the reference level), block/run index (`block`), and trial onset time (`onset`). Temperature was modeled categorically because calibrated thermal levels are discrete and pain-signature expression may be nonlinear across them. Binary pain condition was not included in the same primary nuisance design because it is a deterministic or near-deterministic recoding of the thermal manipulation in this paradigm and can make the estimand unidentified. Trial duration was not included as a nuisance predictor because the target construction already restricts trials to the plateau phase and models plateau duration in the LSS event definition. Level 2 is the primary confirmatory target because it asks whether EEG predicts signature variance beyond measured stimulus level and session structure.

**Level 3 — Rating-residualized sensitivity.** The Level 2 design matrix was augmented with subjective pain rating on the visual analog scale (`vas_final_coded_rating`). Subjective ratings are a criterion measure of pain experience rather than a pure nuisance variable, so this level may remove construct-relevant variance. Level 3 was interpreted only as a sensitivity analysis testing whether EEG predicts residual fMRI signature variance after removing both stimulus/session effects and the subject's own pain report; it was not treated as definitive nuisance-control evidence for pain processing.

Within each level, the nuisance coefficients $\hat{\gamma}$ were estimated exclusively on the training subjects via SVD-based least squares to prevent test-subject leakage:

$$\hat{\gamma} = \underset{\gamma}{\mathrm{argmin}} \; \| y_{train} - Z_{train}\gamma \|_2^2$$

Nuisance predictors were checked for multicollinearity before fitting. The block indicator was encoded as a numeric order term, imposing a linear drift assumption. Dummy-coded block indicators and polynomial trial-order terms were evaluated only as separately reported sensitivity estimands where the design was full rank; they did not replace the primary Level 2 design. The residualized targets for both training and test sets were computed by applying the training-derived coefficients:

$$y_{train}^{resid} = y_{train} - Z_{train}\hat{\gamma}, \qquad y_{test}^{resid} = y_{test} - Z_{test}\hat{\gamma}$$

Conclusions were evaluated with Level 2 as the primary confirmatory estimand. If prediction was significant at Level 1 but not Level 2, the EEG features were interpreted as primarily tracking stimulus or session structure rather than pain-related fMRI signature variance. If prediction survived Level 2 but not Level 3, the prediction was interpreted as overlapping with subjective pain-report variance, not as a failure of the primary pain-relevant hypothesis.

## EEG Feature Extraction
Spectral power features were extracted from band-passed EEG epochs using Morlet wavelet decomposition with frequency-adaptive cycle counts (cycles $= f / 2.0$, bounded between 3.0 and 15.0 cycles) and decimation factor 4. To isolate induced (non-phase-locked) oscillatory activity, the evoked response (ERP) was subtracted from each trial prior to time-frequency decomposition. In the primary analysis, the ERP was estimated as the fold-level average across all training trials regardless of condition. As a sensitivity analysis, condition-specific ERP subtraction—using pain and non-pain condition labels—was also evaluated, acknowledging that condition-specific subtraction removes condition-mean variance and may attenuate condition-related prediction if pain condition is related to the target. In both cases, the ERP was computed exclusively from training-fold trials to prevent cross-validation leakage. Spectral power was log-ratio baseline-corrected against the pre-stimulus window ($-5.0$ to $-0.01$ seconds) and averaged within the active stimulation window ($3.0$ to $10.5$ seconds) to yield one power value per trial, channel, and frequency band. Line noise at 60 Hz was excluded from gamma-band averaging using a $\pm 1.0$ Hz notch; harmonics above the 100 Hz low-pass cutoff were not present in the data and were therefore not excluded. Power features were computed at three spatial resolutions: individual channels, predefined regions of interest (frontal, sensorimotor left/right, temporal left/right, parieto-occipital left/right/midline), and global average.

In addition to power, an exploratory feature set encompassed spectral peak frequency and bandwidth, aperiodic slope and offset (FOOOF/specparam; fixed model), event-related desynchronization and synchronization, band power ratios ($\theta/\beta$, $\theta/\alpha$, $\alpha/\beta$, $\delta/\alpha$, $\delta/\theta$), hemispheric alpha asymmetry, nonlinear complexity measures (permutation entropy, sample entropy, Lempel-Ziv complexity), and oscillatory burst statistics.

## Feature-Based Machine Learning Architecture
A rigorously nested, Leave-One-Subject-Out (LOSO) cross-validation framework was employed to ensure true out-of-sample generalization. The primary confirmatory model was ElasticNet regression on spectral power features, selected for its sparsity-inducing regularization and interpretability in high-dimensional settings. Ridge regression was included as a secondary confirmatory model. Random Forest was designated as exploratory due to its sensitivity to correlated predictors in high-dimensional, low-subject neuroimaging settings and its weaker biological interpretability relative to linear models.

To prevent cross-subject data leakage, a strict sequence of preprocessing steps was applied independently within each outer training fold. Let $X$ represent the trial-by-feature matrix. The primary preprocessing lane was non-transductive: feature medians, means, standard deviations, and variance-threshold decisions were estimated from the outer training subjects only, then applied unchanged to the held-out subject. Missing trials were imputed using the median of the training cohort. Features were standardized to zero mean and unit variance using training-fold statistics, and a variance threshold of $0.0$ was applied to remove features that were constant in the training fold. Session-normalized scaling that uses all held-out subject trials to estimate that subject's mean and standard deviation was excluded from the primary benchmark; if run as a separate calibration analysis, it must be labeled transductive and cannot support the primary claim.

Processed fMRI targets were normalized for model optimization using the Yeo-Johnson power transformation, denoted $\tilde{y} = \mathrm{YJ}(y)$. The transformation parameters were fit on the outer training targets only and applied to the held-out subject. Predictions were inverse-transformed before reporting primary $R^2$, root-mean-squared error, and calibration summaries on the active target scale. Transformed-scale metrics were reported as optimization diagnostics. The ElasticNet objective was:

$$\min_{\beta_0,\beta} \frac{1}{2n} \left\| \tilde{y} - \beta_0 - X\beta \right\|_2^2 + \alpha \rho \|\beta\|_1 + \frac{\alpha(1-\rho)}{2}\|\beta\|_2^2$$

The Ridge objective was:

$$\min_{\beta_0,\beta} \frac{1}{2n} \left\| \tilde{y} - \beta_0 - X\beta \right\|_2^2 + \frac{\alpha}{2}\|\beta\|_2^2$$

Hyperparameters were tuned via a 5-fold inner group cross-validation loop restricted to the training subjects, with validation folds defined at the subject level via GroupKFold to ensure that no validation subject's trials contributed to inner-fold training. The software minimum for running this scheme is 6 analyzable subjects, because every LOSO training fold must contain at least 5 subjects for inner GroupKFold. Confirmatory inference additionally required at least 20 analyzable subjects or a preregistered precision simulation showing a 95% confidence-interval half-width $\leq 0.10$ for the primary out-of-sample $R^2$ under the observed trial structure. Cohorts meeting only the computational minimum were reported as descriptive feasibility analyses. For ElasticNet, the regularization strength $\alpha$ was tuned over the grid $\{0.001, 0.01, 0.1, 1, 10\}$ and the L1 ratio $\rho$ over $\{0.2, 0.5, 0.8\}$, with a maximum of 10,000 iterations and cyclic coordinate descent. For Ridge, $\alpha$ was tuned over $\{0.01, 0.1, 1.0, 10.0, 100.0\}$. Random Forest used 500 bootstrap estimators, with maximum tree depths of $\{5, 10, 20, \text{None}\}$, minimum samples to split of $\{2, 5, 10\}$, and minimum samples per leaf of $\{1, 2, 4\}$.

## Deep Regression Neural Architecture
In parallel, an exploratory deep regression lane was implemented to learn directly from continuous, band-limited EEG dynamics. Because the independent unit for LOSO generalization is the subject rather than the trial, and the cohort size may be modest, the deep network is designated as exploratory unless the subject count is sufficient to justify confirmatory status.

Continuous EEG recordings were band-pass filtered into the target frequency ranges, and instantaneous power was extracted via the analytic signal envelope using the Hilbert transform. The resulting tensors were cropped to the active stimulation window ($3.0$ to $10.5$ seconds), yielding a four-dimensional input tensor of shape $N \times B \times C \times T$ (trials $\times$ bands $\times$ channels $\times$ time). Within each LOSO fold, input tensors were standardized using the channel- and band-specific statistics of the training cohort:

$$\tilde{X}_{n,b,c,t} = \frac{X_{n,b,c,t} - \mu_{b,c}}{\sigma_{b,c}^{*}}$$

where $\mu_{b,c}$ is the training mean and $\sigma_{b,c}^{*}$ is the standard deviation lower-bounded at $10^{-6}$. Target variables were z-scored using training subset statistics.

A custom PyTorch-based convolutional neural network was designed to enforce neurophysiological constraints by sequentially isolating spatial topographies prior to temporal integration. The depthwise spatial convolution utilized a kernel spanning all EEG channels with independent filter groups for each frequency band, producing an output of shape $N \times B \times F \times T$:

$$H^{(1)}_{n,b,f,t} = \mathrm{ELU}\left(\mathrm{BN}\left(\sum_{c} W^{spat}_{b,f,c}\tilde{X}_{n,b,c,t}\right)\right)$$

where $f$ indexes the spatial filters. This was followed by a temporal convolution with kernel length 15 and 8 temporal filters that integrate across bands and time, producing an output of shape $N \times F' \times T$:

$$H^{(2)}_{n,f',t} = \mathrm{ELU}\left(\mathrm{BN}\left(\sum_{b,f,u} W^{temp}_{f',b,f,u} H^{(1)}_{n,b,f,t+u}\right)\right)$$

where $f'$ indexes temporal filters. The latent temporal representation was downsampled via average pooling with factor 8 and passed through a dropout-regularized ($p = 0.25$) fully connected regression head:

$$z_n = \mathrm{Pool}_{8}\left(\mathrm{Dropout}(H^{(2)}_n)\right)$$

$$\hat{y}_n = W_2 \, \mathrm{Dropout}\left(\mathrm{ELU}\left(W_1 \, \mathrm{vec}(z_n) + b_1\right)\right) + b_2$$

The network was optimized using the AdamW algorithm (learning rate $= 0.001$, weight decay $= 0.0001$) minimizing mean squared error over 25 epochs with batch size 32. Twenty percent of the training subjects were reserved as an entirely held-out validation set for patience-based early stopping (patience $= 5$ epochs), and no trials from validation subjects were used during network optimization. Deep-regression folds required at least 10 training subjects so that the validation split contained at least two subjects; folds below this threshold failed deep-regression QC and were not interpreted.

## Evaluation Metrics and Statistical Inference
Model performance was evaluated on the held-out test subject during each LOSO iteration. Primary performance metrics were computed on the active target scale after inverse-transforming predictions where a target transform was used. The coefficient of determination was calculated using the target mean of the training fold ($\bar{y}_{train,f}$) as the zero-skill baseline:

$$R_f^2 = 1 - \frac{\sum_{i \in f}(y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2}{\sum_{i \in f}(y_i - \bar{y}_{train,f})^2}$$

In addition to group-level pooled metrics, subject-wise $R^2$, Pearson correlation, Spearman correlation, and root-mean-squared error are reported with 95% bias-corrected and accelerated (BCa) bootstrap confidence intervals (10,000 resamples) to characterize individual variability in prediction accuracy. Confidence intervals for group-level summaries resampled subjects as the exchangeability unit. Within-subject intervals used run/block-level resampling when at least two runs were available and circular block bootstrap over trial order within run otherwise; trial-level iid bootstrap was not used. Given the small effective sample size at the subject level in LOSO designs, bootstrap confidence intervals may be unstable, and this limitation is acknowledged.

Statistical inference was established via nonparametric permutation testing with 5,000 permutations. To preserve within-run temporal autocorrelation while dismantling trial-wise predictability, target labels were circularly shifted within each run for each subject independently, with a minimum shift distance of 5 trials to prevent near-identity permutations. Circular shifts approximately preserve within-block temporal structure including habituation, carryover, and local drift (Winkler et al., 2014). The number of valid unique circular shifts per subject per run was logged; runs with fewer than 10 valid trials were excluded from permutation inference by prespecified trial-count quality control. For each permutation, the full outer-fold pipeline was refit, including residualization, imputation, scaling, target transformation, feature selection, hyperparameter tuning, and model fitting. As a sensitivity analysis, within-block random shuffling—which preserves block membership but destroys within-block autocorrelation—was also evaluated. The Holm-corrected confirmatory family was defined before model fitting as 2 fMRI targets (NPS, SIIPS1) $\times$ 2 confirmatory linear models (ElasticNet, Ridge) $\times$ the primary Level 2 residualization target $\times$ 3 prespecified spectral-power frequency presets: alpha, beta, and alpha+beta. Gamma and alpha+beta+gamma presets were excluded from the confirmatory family and reported only as exploratory artifact-sensitive analyses. Level 1, Level 3, Random Forest, deep-regression, alternative nuisance designs, and temporal/baseline sensitivity analyses were reported outside this primary family as sensitivity or exploratory analyses.

Computational reproducibility was supported by logging all random seeds (global seed $= 42$), software versions, data hashes, and deterministic computation settings. Exact trial yields, participant inclusions, and selected hyperparameters were programmatically logged to ensure transparent manuscript reporting.

## Nuisance Prediction Reporting
To assess how much of the EEG prediction could reflect measured confounds or pain-report overlap, supplementary models were trained to predict stimulus temperature, trial number, block/run index, and subjective pain ratings from the same EEG features. Strong prediction of stimulus or session variables required caution that the model was tracking task structure. Strong prediction of subjective ratings was interpreted as construct overlap with pain report rather than as a pure nuisance result. The Level 2 residualization framework provides the primary evidence: EEG features must predict stimulus-controlled fMRI pain-signature expression above chance before any pain-relevant interpretation is made.

## Temporal Negative Controls
To assess temporal specificity, models were trained using EEG features extracted from the pre-stimulus window ($-5.0$ to $0.0$ seconds) and the immediate pre-stimulus window ($-0.2$ to $0.0$ seconds) to predict post-stimulus NPS expression. If either pre-stimulus model predicted the pain signature comparably to stimulation-period EEG, the model was interpreted as capturing expectancy, block structure, or subject-level state rather than nociceptive processing. Wrong-lag windows were prespecified as ramp-up ($0.0$ to $3.0$ seconds), late ramp-down/post-stimulation ($10.5$ to $15.0$ seconds), early-shifted active ($1.0$ to $8.5$ seconds), and late-shifted active ($5.0$ to $12.5$ seconds). These windows were evaluated without reselecting frequency bands, channels, or model hyperparameters after inspecting outcomes.
